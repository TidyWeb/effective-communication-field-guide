Communication is not the transmission of words. It is the joint construction of enough shared meaning to understand, decide and act—with a reliable way to discover when that meaning has failed.

The governing idea: choose the human skill and the reliability protocol to match the stakes. A supportive conversation, a design debate and a safety-critical handover should not all be conducted in the same way.

01 / THE SYSTEM

Meaning travels through a system, not a pipe

A message can be perfectly spoken and still fail. People interpret it through different knowledge, expectations, status relationships, emotions, language, sensory access and environments. Effective communicators design for that reality.

02 / ACTIVE LISTENING

Listen to understand, then prove that you did

Active listening is disciplined attention plus visible checking. It is not silent waiting, instant agreement or a performance of empathy. The listener helps the speaker develop, inspect and correct the meaning.

Attend — remove avoidable distraction
Invite — ask an open question
Reflect — name content or feeling
Clarify — test ambiguous words
Summarise — compress the meaning
Verify — “Have I got that right?”
A practical listening sequence

From signal to shared meaning

Begin broadly: “Talk me through what happened.” Follow the speaker’s meaning before imposing your own structure. Reflect observable content—“The delay began after the data refresh”—and, where appropriate, tentatively name emotion: “That sounds frustrating.”

Finish with a concise summary and an invitation to correct it. Correction is evidence that the channel is working, not an embarrassment.

Use more

Open → probe → close

Explore with open questions, deepen with neutral probes, then close only when confirming a fact or commitment.

“What changed?” → “What makes you say that?” → “Was that before Tuesday?”

Use carefully

Paraphrase, not parroting

Restate the meaning in fresh words so the speaker can test your interpretation. Repeating their final sentence may sound mechanical and reveal little understanding.

Avoid

The listening impostors

Interrupting to solve, preparing your reply, cross-examining, topping their story, reassuring too quickly, or asking “why” in a blaming tone.

Worked example · data project

Speaker: “The dashboard never agrees with Finance and I’m tired of defending it.”

Listener: “You are spending time defending the number rather than using it. When do the figures begin to diverge, and what definition does Finance use? I’ll summarise the two definitions when we have them—have I understood the core problem?”

03 / COLLABORATION

Make it safe to contribute—and hard to lose the work

Collaborative communication turns individual knowledge into a shared model. It needs a clear purpose, equitable participation, visible reasoning and a record of decisions—not simply more meetings.

Design the conversation

State the question, desired output, constraints, decision owner and preparation. Invite relevant perspectives, including people affected by the decision.

Make thinking visible

Separate facts, interpretations, options and preferences. Invite quieter voices before the group converges. Summarise areas of agreement and uncertainty.

Close the loop

Record the decision, rationale, owner, next action, deadline, dissent and conditions that would reopen the decision.

Advocacy

State your view and reasoning

“My view is X because the evidence shows Y. The assumption I may be making is Z.”

↔
Inquiry

Explore another view

“What are you seeing that leads you there? What evidence would change either of our minds?”

Psychological safety is not comfort or consensus. It is confidence that relevant questions, errors, concerns and dissent can be raised without ridicule or interpersonal punishment. Pair it with standards and accountability: candour should improve the work.
04 / FEEDBACK

Describe behaviour, reveal impact, open a dialogue

Useful feedback is specific enough to act on and respectful enough to hear. A strong default is SBI with inquiry: Situation, observable Behaviour, Impact—then ask about intent and agree the next experiment.

S

Situation

Locate the event in time and context. Avoid “always” and “never.”

B

Behaviour

Describe what a camera could see or a recorder could hear—not a personality label.

I

Impact

Explain the consequence for the work, people, safety or outcome.

↻

Inquiry & next

Ask about intention and context; agree what to continue, change or test.

Vague judgement

“You were unprofessional in that meeting and need to communicate better.”

The recipient must guess which behaviour was problematic, why it mattered and what “better” means.

Actionable dialogue

“In this morning’s review, you answered three questions before Maya had finished speaking. We lost part of her risk explanation and she stopped contributing. What was happening from your perspective? Next time, please let the question finish and summarise it before responding.”

Reinforcing

Name what to repeat

Positive feedback needs the same precision. Show which behaviour created value so it can become deliberate practice.

Corrective

Make the next move small

Focus on one or two consequential behaviours. Agree an observable experiment and a review point.

Receiving

Listen before deciding

Ask for an example, paraphrase what you heard, separate intent from impact, then say what you will consider or try.

Disagree with the model, not the person

Constructive debate exposes assumptions and improves decisions. Unstructured debate often rewards speed, confidence, status and repetition. A protocol makes evidence and reasoning easier to inspect.

Before positions harden

Write first, discuss second

Ask everyone to record observations, assumptions and options independently. This reduces anchoring on the first or most senior speaker.

During challenge

Do not fake consensus

06 / RELIABILITY PROTOCOLS

Structure communication when omission has consequences

Protocols reduce predictable losses in high-stakes, time-pressured or cross-boundary work. They supplement judgement; they do not replace attention, context or a speak-up culture.

SBAR

Escalate a situation concisely

Useful for a focused update or request, especially across roles. State what is happening now, essential context, your assessment and the action or recommendation required.

Closed-loop communication / check-back

The sender directs a clear message; the receiver repeats their understanding; the sender confirms or corrects it. Use for names, numbers, settings, critical instructions and ownership.

08 / APPLIED EXAMPLES

One skill set, different operating contexts

The core is stable—purpose, listening, checking, repair and a clear next action—but the form changes with risk, audience and workflow.

＋
NHS · multidisciplinary escalation

A patient’s observations are deteriorating

  1. Use SBAR to organise the update and make a specific request.
  2. Use check-back for critical figures and actions.
  3. Use CUS or the local escalation route if the concern is not acknowledged.
  4. Confirm ownership, timing and documentation.
⚙
Engineering · manufacturing shift

A defect pattern crosses a handover

  1. Show the defect, rate, first occurrence and affected batch—not “quality is bad.”
  2. Separate observed facts from suspected cause.
  3. Transfer containment, tests, open risks and owners.
  4. Receiver repeats the immediate controls; sender confirms.
▥
Data & AI · metric dispute

Two teams report different conversion rates

  1. Frame the shared question before defending either number.
  2. Write definitions, grain, filters, time zones and exclusions side by side.
  3. Steelman each interpretation and test against source data.
  4. Record the agreed definition, owner, version and review trigger.
</>
Full stack · code review

A change is difficult to maintain

  1. Use SBI: point to the file/change and its observable effect.
  2. Ask what constraint or intent shaped the approach.
  3. Distinguish required correction from optional preference.
  4. Suggest a small alternative and explain the engineering trade-off.
The same principle across all four

Do not hide the required action inside background. Say what needs attention, show why, invite correction, assign ownership and check the shared understanding.

09 / DIFFICULT MOMENTS

Slow the heat; sharpen the facts

When threat rises, people narrow attention, defend identity and infer hostile intent. Effective communication lowers avoidable threat while preserving the issue that must be addressed.

10 / CURRENT PRACTICE

The frontier is communication that is inclusive, closed-loop and observable

As of July 2026, leading practice is less about inventing new acronyms and more about combining proven interpersonal skills with accessible design, explicit checks and learning from whether communication worked.

01 · Inclusive by design

Access is a core property

Plain language, accessible digital formats, alternative channels, captions, interpreters and communication support are not optional polish. Design around the people who must understand and respond.

02 · Patient and user involvement

Communicate with, not at

TeamSTEPPS 3.0 emphasises active patient and family involvement; NHS shared decision-making and teach-back treat understanding as a collaborative achievement.

03 · Psychological safety plus rigour

Invite voice; test claims

Teams need permission to raise errors and dissent, alongside evidence standards, decision clarity and follow-through.

04 · Hybrid clarity

Conversation plus artefact

Use live interaction for ambiguity and relationship; use durable records for decisions, ownership, definitions and handovers. Neither medium is sufficient alone.

05 · AI-assisted communication

Draft, translate, summarise—then verify

AI can adjust tone, generate accessible variants and summarise discussion, but it can omit dissent, invent agreement and flatten context. Keep a human owner and source record.

06 · Measure the loop

Observe outcomes, not message volume

Use teach-back success, handover defects, reopened decisions, action completion, participation patterns and communication-related incidents to learn where the system fails.

Current-practice caution: named frameworks are aids, not universal standards. Adapt to local policy, professional regulation, clinical or engineering risk controls, language needs and the people involved.
11 / COMMUNICATION CANVAS

Plan the exchange, not just the message

Use this brief for an important conversation, meeting, handover, presentation, written update or difficult piece of feedback.

Purpose

What must be different afterwards?

Understanding, decision, action, relationship, learning or safety.

People

Who needs voice, context or support?

Knowledge, authority, impact, language, accessibility and emotional state.

Essential meaning

What must not be lost?

Key fact, uncertainty, risk, request, boundary or decision.

Mode

Which channel and structure fit?

Conversation, written record, visual, SBAR, feedback model, handover or combination.

Listening

What do I genuinely need to learn?

Prepare open questions and assumptions to test.

Closure

How will shared meaning be checked?

Summary, check-back, teach-back, decision record, owner, deadline and review.
